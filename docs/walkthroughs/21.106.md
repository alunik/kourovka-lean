# How the proof works: Kourovka 21.106

This informal account was generated by **Nilradical v0** after human acceptance of the linked proof snapshot. **Agent-generated exposition; not refereed.** It may simplify details. The exact mathematical claims are the linked Lean statements and proofs; acceptance of that snapshot does not imply human review of this exposition.

Conte and Petschick ask whether every parameter-free first-order formula with one free variable is concise in residually finite groups. Conciseness means that, whenever the set of satisfying elements is finite, the subgroup generated by that set is also finite. Nilradical v0's counterexample is a formula with exactly two values in the integral Heisenberg group. Those values generate an infinite subgroup, while the ambient group is residually finite. The accepted result answers the full formula question negatively; the separate word-conciseness question 21.105 remains outside its scope.

Write [a,b] = a⁻¹b⁻¹ab throughout. The displayed formula is:

> ∃ a,b ∀ g,h ∃ u,v: x = [a,b] ∧ [u,a] = 1 ∧ [v,b] = 1 ∧ [u,b] = [g,h] ∧ [a,v] = [g,h].

## Why the Heisenberg group is useful

The group H is defined on triples of integers with multiplication

> (r,s,t)(r′,s′,t′) = (r+r′, s+s′, t+t′+rs′).

The identity is (0,0,0), and the inverse of (r,s,t) is (−r,−s,rs−t). The [Heisenberg construction](https://github.com/alunik/kourovka-lean/blob/5a6b2c18e326b7b0281f00b64cade629acbfe1f5/Kourovka/Problems/P21_106/Proof/Heisenberg.lean#L19) works over a commutative ring; the counterexample specializes to ℤ. Coordinate calculations prove the group laws. The decisive calculation is `Heisenberg.commutator_eq`: every commutator is (0,0,rs′−sr′).

Put c(n) = (0,0,n), written `central n` in Lean, and let D(a,b) be the determinant of the first two coordinates of a and b. Then [a,b] = c(D(a,b)). Group equations in the formula become integer determinant equations. The third coordinates disappear from these equations, while the map c faithfully records their integer values. This reduction exposes the elementary arithmetic behind the counterexample.

## Forcing exactly two values

Suppose x satisfies the formula, with initial witnesses a and b. Choose g = (1,0,0) and h = (0,1,0), whose commutator is c(1). The universal part supplies u and v satisfying

> D(u,a) = 0, D(v,b) = 0, D(u,b) = 1, D(a,v) = 1.

For any four vectors in two coordinates, the determinant identity is

> D(u,a)D(v,b) − D(u,v)D(a,b) + D(u,b)D(a,v) = 0.

Substituting the four equations gives D(u,v)D(a,b) = 1. An integer with an integer multiplicative inverse is 1 or −1. Therefore x = c(1) or x = c(−1). In [Counterexample.lean](https://github.com/alunik/kourovka-lean/blob/5a6b2c18e326b7b0281f00b64cade629acbfe1f5/Kourovka/Problems/P21_106/Proof/Counterexample.lean#L23), the determinant identity is proved by ring normalization; `formulaCondition_imp` carries its conclusion back to the group element.

The converse needs witnesses that work for every pair g,h. Let ε be 1 or −1, and choose a = (1,0,0), b = (0,ε,0). Given g,h, write d = D(g,h), and take u = (εd,0,0), v = (0,d,0). The two required commuting equations hold because the corresponding coordinate vectors are parallel. The remaining determinants are ε²d and d, both equal to d. Finally [a,b] = c(ε), so these choices satisfy the full formula.

The theorem [formulaCondition_iff](https://github.com/alunik/kourovka-lean/blob/5a6b2c18e326b7b0281f00b64cade629acbfe1f5/Kourovka/Problems/P21_106/Proof/Counterexample.lean#L77) combines both directions. It characterizes the whole value set, rather than merely finding two examples. The [central_injective](https://github.com/alunik/kourovka-lean/blob/5a6b2c18e326b7b0281f00b64cade629acbfe1f5/Kourovka/Problems/P21_106/Proof/Heisenberg.lean#L88) theorem distinguishes c(1) from c(−1), so there are exactly two values.

Their generated subgroup is infinite for a direct reason. We have c(m+n) = c(m)c(n), and every integer power c(1)ⁿ equals c(n). Since c(1) is a formula value, all c(n) belong to the subgroup generated by the values. Injectivity of c embeds ℤ into that actual subgroup. The formal endpoint [formulaCondition_closure_infinite](https://github.com/alunik/kourovka-lean/blob/5a6b2c18e326b7b0281f00b64cade629acbfe1f5/Kourovka/Problems/P21_106/Proof/Counterexample.lean#L102) proves its infinitude. This argument concerns the subgroup generated by a finite set; it does not assert that the subgroup needs infinitely many generators.

## Residual finiteness and the final bridge

The ambient hypothesis also receives a concrete proof. Reduce all three coordinates modulo a positive integer m. Because the multiplication is polynomial in those coordinates, reduction defines a homomorphism to the finite Heisenberg group over ℤ/mℤ. If an integer triple is nonidentity, one coordinate n is nonzero. Choose m = |n|+1. Then n stays nonzero modulo m, so this finite quotient separates the triple from the identity.

The [residual-finiteness instance](https://github.com/alunik/kourovka-lean/blob/5a6b2c18e326b7b0281f00b64cade629acbfe1f5/Kourovka/Problems/P21_106/Proof/Heisenberg.lean#L112) handles each possible nonzero coordinate and supplies Mathlib's `Group.ResiduallyFinite` property. It does not assume a classification theorem about nilpotent groups. The uniform finite-quotient construction covers every nonidentity element.

The short [Solution](https://github.com/alunik/kourovka-lean/blob/5a6b2c18e326b7b0281f00b64cade629acbfe1f5/Kourovka/Problems/P21_106/Solution.lean#L16) first proves `counterexample_values`, identifying the syntax-level value set with the algebraic one. It combines finiteness of those values with infinitude of their closure in `counterexample_not_concise`. The final [not_notebookStatement](https://github.com/alunik/kourovka-lean/blob/5a6b2c18e326b7b0281f00b64cade629acbfe1f5/Kourovka/Problems/P21_106/Solution.lean#L33) applies the proposed universal assertion to H and this formula.
